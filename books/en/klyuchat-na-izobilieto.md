Chapter 1: The Sauce with Golden Sparks

Beneath the spoon, the sauce sparkled.
Billy stirred it slowly, from the rim toward the center. With every turn a single golden spark rose from the bottom, burst on the surface, and vanished. The copper pot sat at the edge of the hearth, where the embers were weakest, and still it simmered harder than it should have.
Not now. There are a hundred people here.
He lifted the spoon and let a drop fall back into the pot. It landed heavy and dark, the color of caramelized onion and old honey. Into the sauce he had stewed thyme from the hills above the city, pink peppercorns from the harbor, and half a cup of the wine the prince drank alone. There was something else in it too, something that had not come from the shelves.
The warmth began in his chest, ran down his arm, and passed into the wood of the spoon. Every pot Billy Moss had stirred in this kitchen kept a little of it, no more than a pinch of salt. Prince Cedric’s guests did not know why the food in his palace was better than at any other table in the kingdom of Gula, and Gula boasted of its tables in front of all seven kingdoms of Estera. They knew only that the head cook was Durand, and they sent their compliments to Durand.
“The quail!” Durand crossed the kitchen with his white beard thrust out before him, shoving apprentices out of his way. “Out they go, in the time it takes to say a prayer! If we’re late, His Highness will have us served up instead of the boar!”
Nobody laughed. Durand said it at every feast, and every time he seemed to mean it.
Dino stood beside Billy with the board of chopped leeks and did not move. He was staring into the pot.
The boy was nineteen, his fingertips roughened by burns, his brown eyes unblinking whenever he was learning something. Since spring, Dino had watched Billy’s hands more than his own. Once Billy had caught him dipping a finger into the sauce when he thought no one was looking. Afterward Dino had stood over the pot for a long while, head tilted, listening for something.
“Master.” Dino swallowed. “There was a moment when your sauce was sparkling. I’m not crazy, am I?”
“Of course you’re crazy. All cooks are crazy, or they’d have become clerks.” Billy did not stop the spoon. “That’s the fat popping, boy. Get enough heat under it and it pops and flashes. Hand me the leeks.”
Dino passed him the board. His hands shook a little.
Billy tipped the leeks into the pot, and the sparks died beneath the green. His shoulders loosened. His thumb slid along his forefinger, an old habit his hand kept on its own, as though it were crumbling salt.
“I’ve been watching for weeks,” Dino said quietly. “When you lean over the pot, it gets warmer. And it isn’t the fire.”
“It’s the fire. Everything in this kitchen comes from the fire. You, me, and Durand’s beard.”
Dino smiled, because that was what one did. His eyes stayed on the pot.
The back door opened, and Feran came in on the wind from the courtyard. He wore the leather breastplate of a captain of Cedric’s guard, his helmet tucked under his arm. He walked past the oven without asking, reached into the basket of warm loaves, and tore off half a loaf.
“Captain.” Durand eyed him from across the kitchen. “That bread is for the guests.”
“I’m a guest. I guard you from them.” Feran took a bite and turned to Billy. “I’ll be at the great door till the end. The prince wants a captain on the door as long as his brother’s at the table.”
“Have you eaten anything today besides that?”
“I’m eating now.”
“Sit down a moment. I’ve got broth from last night.”
“I don’t have time for broth, Billy.” Feran finished the bread and brushed the crumbs off his breastplate. “And don’t look at me like that. I’ve been a captain for two years. I can stand at a door on an empty stomach.”
Billy was already ladling some into a bowl. He set it on the edge of the table, right by Feran’s hand, and said nothing.
Feran looked at the bowl, then at him. He sighed, picked it up, and drank it in three swallows, standing, his helmet under his arm. When he was done, his mouth twisted. The broth was good, but Feran did not like being fed like a child.
“Oswin’s at his little spoon again,” he said quietly as he set the bowl down. “I saw him on the stairs. He touched his doublet pocket on every step.”
“Let him touch it. He’ll eat here.”
“He’ll eat.” Feran put on his helmet. “And I’d rest easier if you kept to the kitchen tonight.”
The door closed behind him. The bowl was empty, and through Billy’s chest spread that light, warm release that always came when someone drank something he had poured. He had put nothing of himself into the broth. He had only warmed it.
Billy Moss looked forty-five. His dark hair was shot with silver threads that no barber could explain. For fifteen years a new one had appeared every time he put something of himself on someone’s plate. He had long since stopped counting them.
The liveried servants waited in a line by the door with their empty trays. Billy went down the row of plates and spooned sauce over the breast of each bird. At the thirty-second plate he stopped. The quail there was smaller than the others and had a scorched wing. Whoever got it would notice. They would leave half of it.
Billy held his palm over the plate and kept it there. The warmth sank from his palm into the meat. The bird’s skin drew taut and glistened. He lifted his hand and the floor swayed beneath him, so he braced himself against the table and waited.
Now everyone will be happy.
“Come, Billy.” Durand tugged him by the sleeve toward the small door to the hall. “Let’s watch them eat.”
Behind the door stood a screen of carved walnut, and behind the screen there was room enough for two. From there the head cook and his assistant could watch the feast without the feast watching them. Durand stood in front, hands folded over his belly. Billy stood behind him and looked over his shoulder.
The hall was long, its windows facing the harbor, and in its two hearths whole logs were burning. The table ran down the middle from end to end beneath three rows of candles. Outside, the sea wind chased the first dry leaves of autumn across the courtyard, but in here it was hot and smelled of wax and roasting meat.
At the head of the table sat King Theodor’s sons. The king had not left his bed since summer, and the feast in his younger son’s palace was a feast without a king. Prince Oswin, the firstborn, wore royal blue; his face was narrow and his eyes a cold blue. Prince Cedric, three years younger, wore dark red. His eyes were dark, and they laughed before his mouth did.
To Oswin’s right sat the king’s chaplain, a thin man in a gray cassock. On his chest, on a leather cord, hung a piece of blue glass as large as an egg. The candle flames shone through it and fell across the tablecloth in bluish stains.
Billy did not look at the chaplain for long. He did not like looking at the blue glass.
Across the hall, by its great door, stood Feran, one hand on his sword hilt. He watched the hall the way Billy watched a hearth, all of it at once. Fifteen years ago, at a caravan, Billy had pulled him out of the hands of bandits, not long after he himself had woken with no memory. Back then Feran had been a nine-year-old from the lower quarters, with no father and no shoes. Now he was twenty-four, a captain, and still stealing bread from the oven.
The quail went out.
The guests reached for them all at once. A woman closed her eyes. Two wine merchants from the harbor traded a look over their plates, and one of them nodded. Oswin took a bite and put down his fork to say something to the chaplain, then picked it up again.
Billy let out his breath.
“Durand!” Cedric raised his cup toward the screen. He knew where the cooks stood. Everyone in the palace knew. “Come out and let us have a look at you, man. My father’s been living on porridge for three months, and you feed us as if it were a coronation.”
Durand straightened his apron and stepped out in front of the screen. He bowed low, then once more toward Oswin, and his white beard came to rest on his chest.
“Your Highness flatters me. I have kept that sauce a secret for twenty years.”
Billy watched his back and smiled. The sauce had been made that morning. Durand had not even tasted it. Billy did not mind. The faces at the table were the faces he had wanted to see, and whose name they spoke afterward did not matter. What mattered was that they ate.
Oswin did not raise his cup to Durand. He slipped two fingers into his doublet pocket, felt for something there, and drew them out empty. His face was pale, with red blotches high on the cheekbones, and the hand that picked up his fork again was trembling.
Feed him. It’ll pass.
Midway down the table, an old lord with puffy eyelids cut into his quail, tasted it twice, and with his fingertips nudged the plate toward the center of the table, just far enough that it was no longer under his nose. Then he turned to his neighbor.
Something clenched in the pit of Billy’s stomach.
For an instant something else flashed before his eyes. A small hand, a child’s, sticky with juice, was pushing away a ripe fig. The fruit rolled across something white and stopped. Billy did not remember whose hand it had been or where. The clench in the pit of his stomach was the same.
He stepped around the screen.
“Where are you going?” Durand caught him by the elbow without turning around. “You belong in the kitchen, not at the table.”
“He didn’t like it.”
“Lord Evar hasn’t liked anything in twenty years. He has gallstones.” Durand nudged him back. “Go and see to the boar.”
The boar was ready. Billy knew it, but he went back to the kitchen anyway.
The sauceboats for the head of the table stood on a tray of their own, silver, with lids shaped like seashells. Billy filled them himself. Then he held his palm over the first one and kept it there longer than he had over the quail. The princes had to be the most pleased of all. Oswin had eaten, but he had put down his fork.
The sparks rose above the sauce, and this time they did not die at once.
Behind him, Dino’s knife stopped chopping. Billy did not turn. He closed the lid, and the warmth stayed inside while his arm went cold to the elbow.
“Take them out,” he told the servants.
When he got back behind the screen, no one in the hall was eating anymore. They were talking.
“…and I will not keep silent at my own table,” Oswin was saying. He did not shout. He spoke in an even voice, and it carried all the way to the foot of the table. “There are heretics three days’ ride from this city. They brew potions in the mountains, they preach across the hills, and they take the bread out of the villages’ mouths. The Order of Azur has known it for years.”
The Order of Azur, which in Gula was also called the Order of the Prophet Elijah after its founder, had its own temple here in Satura and its own confessor at the sick king’s side. The confessor sat to Oswin’s right and kept silent. The glass on his chest swayed as he breathed.
“The Order knows a great many things.” Cedric had not put down his fork. “It knows what the harbor tithe comes to. It knows when our father makes his confession, too. It just doesn’t know why the villages in the hills are starving. Does it, Father?”
The chaplain bowed his head and smiled at the tablecloth. He did not answer.
“The Order has eyes where we have only candles.” Oswin touched his doublet pocket. “When I take the throne, I will give it the seat on the council that is its due.”
“When you take the throne.” Cedric smiled at the guests, not at his brother. “Did you hear him? My brother is already handing out chairs on the council, and our father is still breathing. Next he’ll tell us that fallen angels are cooking our supper.”
Someone laughed. The laughter ran down the table and stopped at Oswin.
Billy gripped the edge of the screen. His fingers found the carving and sank into it.
“Laugh,” Oswin said. “The Order has already caught three of them. They walk among us with human faces. They eat with us. And Azur sees every one of them through the blue, brother, however much you laugh.”
The blue glass on the chaplain’s chest caught the flame of a candle.
Somewhere in the pit of Billy’s stomach, the fear turned hot.
A glass on the table shattered.
It was a tall glass of red wine standing before a young woman at the foot of the table. It burst into a star, and the wine splashed across the tablecloth and over her sleeves. The woman shrieked and leapt to her feet. The man beside her leapt up after her and knocked over his chair.
“Must have been cracked,” someone said.
“Cracked,” several voices echoed, relieved to have a word for it.
Billy looked down at his palms. They were warm, warmer than they should have been. He drew them away from the carved screen. For a moment it had seemed to him that the wood under his fingers was beginning to smoke.
A woman with rubies in her gray hair rose from her place beside Cedric. She did not hurry. She waited for the servants to cover the wine with cloths, and only then did she lift her cup.
“Leave politics to the council, dear princes,” said Countess Marguerite. “Here we serve dessert. But the boar comes first.”
The laughter returned, louder than the joke deserved. Oswin pursed his lips and reached for his cup. Cedric nodded to the countess as though she had done him a favor he meant to remember.
The boar came in on four platters, with the silver sauceboats alongside.
Billy’s eyes went looking for Oswin. The servant in front of the prince lifted the seashell lid, and the sauce spilled over the meat, dark and glossy. Steam rose from the plate. Oswin bent his face over it and closed his eyes.
Something at the back of Billy’s neck eased.
That was when he saw Feran.
The captain was not watching the prince’s plate. His hand had left the sword hilt and frozen in midair. He was looking at something behind Oswin’s chair.
Cedric’s cupbearer stood there, a thin man in gray leather gloves. He was pouring wine into Oswin’s cup from a long-necked jug. With his free hand, low, at the rim of the cup, he tipped in something from a vial no bigger than a thumb. The vial vanished into his sleeve before the wine had stopped flowing.
The last drop to fall from it was dark red and thick as honey.
Billy knew that color. Half the court knew it. It was the Dew of the Spear. The fathers of the monastery of the Spear brewed it up in the hungry hills and sold it in little vials stoppered with wax, sweet and smelling of burnt sugar. At court they drank it for vigor, for sleep, and for forgetting. Oswin had been taking it for years, from a silver spoon, every morning and every evening, and believed no one knew. Everyone knew.
If someone poured him a double measure, Oswin would never notice the taste.
The cupbearer stepped back. Feran glanced toward Cedric. Cedric was talking with the countess and looking at no one.
Oswin took a bite of the boar. He took a second, faster. He reached for his cup and drained it to the bottom.
The warmth Billy had left in the sauce was still his. He could feel it the way one feels a thread tied around a finger: far off, at the end of the table, inside Oswin. It was burning. And where it burned, something sweet and dark seized hold of it and dragged it down toward the belly, and turned it into hunger.
No. Stop. He’s had enough.
Billy did not know how to call it back. He had never once had to call anything back.
He tried all the same. He closed his eyes and reached for the thread with the same motion he used to lift the lid off a pot that was boiling over. He pulled. The thread would not come. At the other end, something held it in its teeth, something sweet and dark, and the harder Billy pulled, the more warmth drained out of his chest along the thread and into Oswin. His knees went weak. The taste of burnt sugar filled his mouth, and it was not his own.
He let go before he could fall.
When he opened his eyes, Durand was gripping his arm above the elbow.
“What’s the matter with you? You’re white as a sheet.”
“It’s hot.”
“It’s hot,” Durand agreed. He let go of him, his eyes already back on the hall.
Oswin was tearing at the boar with his fingers. Grease ran down his chin. He crammed meat into his mouth before he had swallowed the last of it and reached for the chaplain’s plate. The chaplain drew back. Oswin snatched at the platter of plums, then bread, then meat again, and his hands shook so badly that half of it fell onto the tablecloth.
One by one the guests fell silent, from the head of the table down to the foot.
“Your Highness,” the chaplain said quietly.
Oswin did not hear him. His jaw kept working. His eyes were wide and wet, and there was nothing in them but hunger.
Then the firstborn of Gula pitched forward and fell face-first into his plate.
The plate cracked under his weight. Sauce splattered across the blue velvet. Oswin’s body shook in great heaving jolts, and his fingers went on groping across the tablecloth, searching for bread.
A woman screamed. Chairs scraped across the stone. The chaplain bent over Oswin and held the blue glass up before his face.
“Azur sees. A physician. Now.”
Cedric did not get up. He laid down his fork, wiped his fingers on his napkin, and only then rose, slowly, and stepped toward his brother.
Billy could not move. In front of him Durand was muttering something and tracing the sign of the Blue Eye over his chest, and Billy watched Oswin’s hands, still searching for bread.
Across the hall, across its whole length, through the candle smoke and the people running back and forth between them, Feran raised his eyes and found Billy’s.
Cedric was no longer looking at his brother. He was looking at Feran by the door. Then his gaze traveled across the hall to where the captain was looking: to the small kitchen door and the walnut screen, above which the cook’s face showed.
 
Chapter 2: One Hour

Two physicians came, one of them still in his nightshirt beneath his cloak.
They turned Oswin onto his back on the floor, between the table and the hearth. Someone had pushed a folded tablecloth under his head, and it was already wet with sauce and spittle. The prince was no longer chewing. His jaw chattered. His fingers scraped at the stone floor, searching for bread that no one would give him.
The chaplain knelt beside him. He held the blue glass over the prince’s eyes with both hands and whispered. Oswin stared up through the glass at the candles and did not blink.
Billy stood behind the screen and could not tear his eyes from those fingers on the floor.
The guests had drawn back toward the windows. Countess Marguerite stood alone beside Oswin’s empty chair, holding a wine-soaked napkin and not knowing where to put it down. The two merchants from the harbor were slipping toward the door, heads bowed, and Feran let them go without a glance.
Cedric knelt on the other side of his brother. He took the hand that was scraping at the floor and held it in his own.
“Hush, Oswin. Hush.” His voice was gentle, and it carried all the way to the screen. “I’m here.”
Oswin whimpered and pulled his hand away. Once more he went searching for bread.
The chaplain reached out a finger and wiped a drop of sauce from the prince’s chin. He raised the finger before the blue glass and studied the drop through it for a long while, up close, the way a goldsmith studies a stone. Then he wiped his finger on his cassock and looked toward the screen.
Billy stepped back. He did not know whether the chaplain had seen him. He knew only that the sauce on that finger was his sauce.
“Whose sauce is this?” The chaplain did not raise his voice. He was asking Cedric, who still held his brother.
“My palace’s,” Cedric said. “I’ll tell you whose. Tonight.”
“Inside.” Durand pulled Billy back toward the kitchen. “Inside, I said. We’ve seen nothing.”
The kitchen was full and silent. The apprentices had stopped turning the spits and stood with their arms at their sides. Fat from the boar dripped into the embers and hissed. No one skimmed it.
Dino stood by the hearth, next to the copper pot, watching Billy.
Billy went to the table, picked up a knife, and began chopping onions. No onions were needed. His hands needed something to do before they began to shake in front of everyone.
The door to the yard opened without a knock.
A sergeant of Cedric’s guard came in, a sword at his hip and a face that wished it were somewhere else. Behind him stood the cupbearer in the gray gloves. He did not come in. He stayed on the threshold, hands clasped behind his back, and surveyed the kitchen from corner to corner.
“His Highness asks who cooked the sauce,” the sergeant said.
Durand stepped forward before anyone else could open his mouth.
“Not me!” His voice rang up to the rafters. “The quail, yes, the boar, yes, but I haven’t stirred that sauce since summer! I gave it to him, to Moss, he brews it himself, I’ve told him so. How many times have I said he whispers over his pots? Ask anyone you like!”
No one had ever heard Durand say anything of the kind. In all the time Billy had been making the sauce, Durand had never breathed a word about it; he had only pocketed the bows. Now he was shouting and pointing with his whole arm.
Dino looked at Durand, then at Billy.
“I made it,” Billy said.
The sergeant nodded. He did not know what to do with the answer, so he looked back toward the threshold. The cupbearer was looking at Billy. He looked at him for a long time, from his graying hair to the knife in his hand, and then he turned and left. The sergeant followed him out.
Durand sat down on a barrel and wiped his face with his apron.
“Don’t let me hear you say anything different,” he muttered, to no one.
Billy put down the knife.
He went into the flour pantry, because no one was there, and leaned with both hands on a sack. A fine white dust rose beneath his palms. It smelled of dryness and of winter.
The pantry door opened and closed again. Feran stood with his back against it.
“Did you see?” Billy asked.
“I saw.” Feran was breathing slowly through his nose. He had taken off his helmet, and his hair was wet with sweat. “I saw Gernot’s hand. He tipped something from a vial into the cup while he was pouring. And Cedric saw that I’d seen.”
“Gernot?”
“The cupbearer. Did you see how he looked at you just now?” Feran crossed his arms, then let them drop. “Listen to me. Oswin will live or he won’t, I don’t know. The physicians say it’s the dew, and that’s true. The chaplain says it’s the sauce. And that’s true too, isn’t it?”
Billy did not answer.
“Isn’t it, Billy?”
“The sauce was warm.” Billy lifted his hands from the sack and looked at them. Flour clung to his palms. “I gave him more. I wanted him to eat.”
Feran closed his eyes for a moment.
“All right. Don’t tell that to anyone else.” He opened them. “The chaplain asked for a quill and wax. He’s writing an order in the clerk’s room off the hall. For the arrest of the cook who made the sauce. I heard him dictating. He says there were sparks in the sauce, and that at the table of the royal house, sparks like that are looked at through the blue.”
A chill settled in the pit of Billy’s stomach.
“And Cedric will sign it?”
“Cedric doesn’t need to sign anything. The chaplain is the king’s confessor. His seal opens the gates of Satura faster than the prince’s does.” Feran leaned toward him. “And Cedric will let the chaplain take you. He’ll be glad of it. The cook is guilty, the cook poisoned Oswin, and the captain who saw Gernot gets sent to the border tomorrow, or into some ditch. Cedric wants both of us silent, Billy. And the dead keep silent best.”
“You’re his captain.”
“That’s how I know.” Feran opened the pantry door and looked out. “We leave tonight. Both of us. I’m going down to the gate to see how far the order has gotten. You stay here and don’t move.”
“Feran.”
The captain turned.
“The second table hasn’t eaten yet,” Billy said. “The gate guards, the grooms, the chaplain’s men who came with him. They’ve been sitting in the servants’ hall since sundown. If someone could carry something out to them.”
Feran looked at him in silence.
“The chaplain’s men, Billy. The men who’ll be coming to take you.”
“They’re hungry.”
“Don’t move from here.” Feran shut the door behind him.
Billy stayed in the dark, among the flour. Through the boards he could hear the kitchen: quiet at first, then louder and louder, because Durand had stopped lamenting and started shouting. He heard someone skimming the fat from the embers, someone asking whether to carry out the dessert, Durand saying that nobody wanted dessert with the prince lying on the floor, and then telling them to carry it out anyway, because princes might ask for it.
The servants’ hall was on the other side of the wall. Some thirty people sat there, waiting for their supper since sundown, and no one gave them a thought.
His thumb rubbed against his forefinger.
Feran came back after the logs over the hearths had been changed. He did not come into the pantry. He beckoned from the kitchen door, and Billy went out to him in the wood passage.
“We have an hour,” Feran said quietly.
“What do you mean, an hour?”
“The order reached the palace gate. The old man from the chancery checks it there, the one who sits in the booth by the portcullis and examines the seal on everything that goes in and out. The one whose fingers are black with ink up to the knuckles.” Feran shrugged. “He said the seal was struck crooked and the edge of the wax doesn’t catch the cord. He sent it back. By the time the chaplain seals it again and it comes back down to the gate, an hour will have passed. The guard told me. The old man has sent things back for correction before, no matter whose. Once he sent back an order from the king himself.”
Billy stared at Feran.
“Why—”
“I didn’t ask why.” Feran looked him in the eye. “Don’t you ask either. It’s one hour. They won’t give us more.”
Billy did not ask. He nodded, but Feran had already turned his back to him and was counting on his fingers as he looked down the passage: horses, money, a way out.
“Then why are you coming? You could stay. Say you never saw a thing.”
“Cedric looked at me in the hall, Billy. He knows I saw.” Feran did not turn around. “Besides, who’s going to get you out? In four years you haven’t left this palace through a single door that doesn’t smell of onions.”
“Every door smells of onions if you’re a cook.”
Feran laughed once, a short breath through his nose.
“Our way out is the service gate to the barrel yard. Old Hubert is on it. He knows you. He knows me too. I’ll do the talking. You’ll keep quiet.” Now he turned. “You have just enough time to fetch your cloak.”
Billy did not go for his cloak. He went to the bread basket.
He took out two round loaves, then a third. He wrapped them in a clean cloth together with a wedge of cheese from the pantry and half a sausage from the hook above the hearth. His hands worked quickly and surely, as though this work at least was still his own. He put the bundle in a sack and placed the sack in Feran’s hands.
“What’s this?”
“You haven’t eaten since noon. A bowl of broth isn’t a meal.”
“Billy, we have an hour.”
“That’s why. Later there won’t be time.” Billy pulled the drawstring of the sack tight around Feran’s wrist, the way you tie something on a child so he won’t drop it. “You’ll eat it on the road.”
Feran looked at the sack on his wrist. He opened his mouth, then closed it. He did not take it off.
Durand watched them from the other end of the kitchen, still sitting on his barrel. He said nothing. When Billy passed him, the head cook reached under his apron, took something out, and pressed it into Billy’s hand. It was a small paring knife with a horn handle, the one Durand never let anyone touch.
“I never saw you,” Durand said without raising his head. “And the sauce isn’t mine.”
“I know it isn’t yours.”
Durand nodded at the floor. His beard was trembling.
Billy tucked the knife into his belt and went to the hearth, to the copper pot.
The sauce had gone cold at the edge of the embers. A thin skin had formed on top. Billy skimmed it off with the spoon and stirred. The sauce warmed faster than the embers could have warmed it.
A golden spark rose from the bottom. Then another.
“Dino.”
The boy was at his side before he had finished saying his name.
“Take this.” Billy gripped the pot by both handles and set it in Dino’s hands. It was heavy, and Dino held on with all his strength. “There’s bread in the basket. There’s boar on the second grill, the part that never got served. Take it to the servants’ hall. Serve everyone. And the sauce, a spoonful to a plate, no more.”
“Master…”
“Don’t let the second table go hungry. They’ve been sitting there since sundown.”
Dino looked at the pot in his hands. Then he raised his eyes.
“Where are you going?”
Billy put his hands on the boy’s shoulders. They were narrow, still a boy’s shoulders, and they tensed beneath his palms.
“Remember me the way I am now,” he said.
“I don’t understand.”
“You don’t need to.” Billy squeezed his shoulders. “If anyone asks about me, tell them what you know. Only what you know. Understand? Don’t lie for my sake. You’re no good at lying, your ears give you away.”
Dino’s ears turned red.
“The sauce,” Dino said quietly. “It had sparks, didn’t it? Before, too. I saw them back then—”
“Grease. I told you.”
Dino swallowed. He did not believe it, but he nodded.
“Will you teach me? When you come back?”
“I’ll teach you.”
Billy let go of his shoulders and stepped back. Dino’s face was red all the way to his ears, and his eyes were wet and unblinking, the way they were when he was learning something. Billy turned away before he could see any more.
In the passage Feran was waiting for him with a cloak that was not Billy’s.
“Yours hangs on the peg by the oven, and everyone knows it,” Feran said, throwing it over Billy’s shoulders. “This one belongs to a groom. He’ll curse me tomorrow.”
The side passages of the palace were narrow and low, with smoke-blackened ceilings, meant for the servants and the firewood. Billy knew them by their smells: here, wet wool from the laundry; there, horse sweat from the stairs down to the stables; farther on, cold ash from the bread ovens. For four years he had walked them carrying trays and baskets. He had never walked them empty-handed. He did not know what to do with his hands.
At a bend Feran raised a hand and stopped.
Ahead, from the main corridor, came footsteps, heavy ones, two men. Feran pressed himself against the wall, and Billy pressed in beside him. Two of the chaplain’s guards crossed the side passage with a lantern, without looking aside. One of them was talking.
“…by morning. He says once it’s sealed, we take in the kitchen.”
“All of it?”
“The cooks. The helpers. Anyone who had a hand in the stirring.”
The footsteps faded. Feran did not move until they had faded completely. Then he set off, and Billy followed.
The helpers. Anyone who had a hand in the stirring.
The thought stopped him in mid-stride.
“Dino,” Billy said.
“What about Dino?”
“You heard him. The helpers.” Billy had already turned back toward the kitchen. “I gave him the sauce. I put it in his hands myself. They’ll catch him with the pot in his hands.”
Feran caught him by the elbow.
“They’ll question him. He’ll say you cooked it and he served it. That’s the truth, and they’ll let him go with it.” His fingers tightened. “If you go back, they’ll take you both.”
“You don’t know they’ll let him go.”
“I don’t.” Feran did not let go of his elbow. “I know that if they find you in that kitchen an hour from now, you’ll both burn on the same fire. Is that what you want to give him?”
Billy stood in the passage, facing the kitchen. From there came the smell of cooling boar, of onions, of the sauce. For as long as he could remember, he had always walked toward that smell. Now he had to walk away from it.
“You’re deciding for me,” he said.
“Tonight, yes.” Feran tugged him onward. “Tomorrow we can fight about it.”
Billy went. Feran did not let go of his elbow until the end of the passage.
The service gate was a low oak door in the barrel yard, beneath an arch. Beside it stood a booth with a small brazier, and in the booth sat Hubert, an old watchman with a single eyebrow grown clear across his forehead, who for four years had opened this door for the flour carts and the fish carts. Hubert stood up when he saw Feran. He saw Billy behind him too, and the eyebrow went up.
“Captain.”
“I’m going out on the prince’s business,” Feran said. “With a man.”
“I have no order for anyone to leave, Captain.”
“The order comes from me.” Feran reached into his belt and took out his purse. He did not hand it over. He set it on the sill of the booth, beside the brazier, and took his hand away. “For firewood, Hubert. It’s cold tonight.”
Hubert looked at the purse, then at Billy. He looked at him for a long time, longer than the cupbearer had. His eyes passed over Billy’s nose, his graying hair, and the groom’s cloak that did not fit him. He was memorizing.
“It’s cold,” Hubert said. He pocketed the purse and drew back the bolt.
The night outside was damp and smelled of the sea. The wind off the harbor chased dry leaves across the cobbles of the yard and heaped them in the corners among the barrels. The sky hung low and starless. Somewhere below, in the city, a dog was barking.
Billy stepped outside and stopped.
