- 1 -

The command center of the Rising Sun had long since lost the appearance of a battleship's bridge. Weeks ago, the bulkheads had come down to make room for additional workstations. Twenty-six consoles, arranged in three concentric semicircles, sifted through data streams from dozens of systems. Cables, left outside the panels due to a lack of time, crawled across the floor like the exposed tendons of an organism that had grown too fast. Blue and orange glows from the holographic projectors painted the operators' faces as they guided the fleets across eight distinct sectors.
The tactical map dominated everything. When Rami first saw it eleven months ago, it fit on a single screen. Now it filled the entire ceiling—a three-dimensional miniature projection of the galaxy. Every star glowed like a white dot, every habitable world a small sphere colored according to its political allegiance. Blue for the Empire. Red for Koriyama. Gray for the neutral systems waiting to see the outcome of the clash before picking a side.
Three months ago, red had claimed nearly half the volume. Now it had withered to a handful of isolated zones—clusters of fortified systems huddled around the core of the rebellion. The blue washed over them relentlessly, like a tide ignoring a headwind. But the red still persisted—vivid and menacing in its isolation.
Rami stood before the map, her palms resting on the command console. Her face had sharpened, her childhood softness gone. Her cheekbones stood out, and her jaw remained still and tight. Fine lines nestled around her eyes, lines that had nothing to do with age—they were the scars of insomnia and responsibilities shouldered in the dead of night while others slept. Her gaze swept over the projection methodically, as if reading a book whose text rewrote itself every second.
Every red patch represented an equation waiting for a solution. Every blue zone—a promise she had to keep.
Dad never mentioned how lonely it is here in the center. Everyone expects answers. Everyone believes I know what I'm doing.
Her fingers pressed against the cold metal of the console, then abruptly let go.
"Commander."
Admiral Jin approached and activated his personal hologram with a sharp flick of his wrist. A tall man with imposing shoulders, he maintained the measured confidence of a veteran who had spent his life in the narrow corridors of warships. His rusty beard, graying at the edges, betrayed priorities far removed from vanity. The dark shadows under his eyes had become such a permanent fixture that Rami no longer registered them.
"Recovery is ahead of initial projections," he reported, unfolding a data table in blue and green. "The shipyards in the Hoshi system have shifted to a three-shift rotation. The Phoenix dock is wrapping up repairs on two hulls within days. The Katana-class destroyers will enter service by the end of the week."
Rami nodded, her eyes fixed on the strategic map.
"What is the status of the pilot roster?"
Jin tapped his fingers over the hologram. Everyone at this level knew the truth, but voicing it always carried a heavy weight.
"In the last three engagements, we lost forty-seven percent of our veterans." He paused for a moment. "The cadets from the latest graduating class are showing high morale. They have excellent reflexes and solid theoretical training, but their combat experience is measured in hours."
He didn't finish. Rami knew the rest. Kids of sixteen or seventeen—an age meant for navigation lectures at the academy, not fighter cockpits. The experienced pilots who should have filled those seats were now just names on the list she carried in her memory.
"And what are their spirits like?" she asked softly.
Jin deactivated the table and crossed his arms.
"High. Inexplicably high after what happened at Kiryu." He looked directly at her, without evasion. "The people believe in you, Commander. They talk of Rami the Invincible. Of the princess who never backs down."
"Rami the Invincible." The words left a bitter taste. If they only knew how often she woke in the dark with a racing pulse, the names of the dead etched on the inside of her eyelids. Kimura. Harada. Noda. Captain Yamada with his porcelain cup. Three thousand five hundred casualties from Kiryu. Girls and boys she had sent into battle because there was no other choice.
"But the people are exhausted," Jin added more quietly. His arms dropped. "Eleven months, Commander. Families are suffering. Resources are dwindling. Even the most loyal worlds are no longer asking 'if,' but 'when.' When will all this end?"
Rami turned to him. There was no surprise in her eyes—she had sensed the fatigue long before Jin put it into words. She saw it in the one-minute delay as the technicians changed shifts. In the way Sakamoto pressed his temples, thinking he was alone. In the three cups of tea she herself drank before she could focus on the morning reports.
"Soon, Admiral," she replied. "Sooner than you think."
She stepped up to the command console and touched two of the sensors. The lights in the center went out. The tactical map unfolded, swallowing the ceiling, walls, and floor—now the two of them stood among the stars, immersed in the heart of the galaxy.
"Kenjiro. Akira."
Kenjiro detached himself from the analysts and walked toward her. His left arm still rested in a splint. The fracture from the battle at Kiryu had calcified, but Dr. Ishida insisted on immobilization. Unlike most warriors, Kenjiro listened to his doctors. His face bore the imprint of the last few months—a thin scar along his jaw from the Dancing Shadow and deep furrows around his eyes—but his gaze remained the same. Dark, calm, having walked through enough fire to be unfazed by anything.
Akira approached from the other side. He moved slowly, with the caution of a man who was just learning to trust his body again. He had left the stasis pod five weeks ago. Regeneration had restored his tissues, his brain activity had normalized, and Dr. Ishida had cleared him for light duty. His face had thinned, his cheekbones jutting sharply against his pale skin. His hands, however, were steady, and his eyes held that depth characteristic of those who had glimpsed the other side.
"Attention, please," Akira said, preempting Rami. His fingers routed data to the central display. "I have analyzed the last three hundred hours of intelligence traffic from Koriyama's peripheral zones. I noticed an anomaly."
"Wait. Give us context first," Rami interrupted with a barely noticeable smile. "Let everyone see the big picture."
She pointed to the deep red zones.
"Akira, explain what you see."
He studied the map with narrowed eyes.
"Koriyama has shrunk his perimeter," he concluded. "He's pulled back his vanguard and concentrated his power at three strategic points. Ashikaga—his capital and stronghold. And here—at Takada and Mitsubishi." He marked the two systems. "Sensor data confirms a massive transfer of resources to these three nodes. Thousands of tons of raw materials daily. They are fortifying at an accelerated pace."
"Classic echelon defense," Kenjiro chimed in. His voice was low and even. "Three positions, short lines of communication, mutual cover."
"Their goal is to force us into sequential attacks until we are completely bled dry."
"Exactly what he expects," Rami concluded. She stepped into the hologram, the starlight washing over her shoulders. "He expects us to throw the fleet against his fortifications. To lose ships, pilots, and time while he prepares his counterblow." She looked at the two men. "But we are not going to give him what he hopes for."
Her hand rose. Her finger bypassed the crimson clusters and traced small gray dots on the periphery of enemy territories—systems so insignificant that their names were missing from the main navigation chart.
The image sharpened. Small worlds connected by thin logistical lines to Koriyama's main bases. Nondescript. Peripheral. Capillaries feeding a vital organ.
"Here," Rami said, marking the first point. "The Hara system. A second-tier shipyard. At first glance, it's unimportant, but the data Akira extracted from the intel traffic suggests otherwise. It provides spare parts for half of Koriyama's fleet. Without it, his ships' repair cycles are extended by nearly a month."
Her finger moved.
"Shibuya. A communications hub. A small station with barely twenty personnel. However, it coordinates the data flow between the three central systems. If we take it out, Ashikaga, Takada, and Mitsubishi lose real-time connectivity. They'll have to switch to delayed relay channels, which will degrade their coordination by at least sixty percent."
"And here," she finished, pointing to the third target. "The mine at Yamada. Rare-earth elements for his ships' energy cores."
Jin leaned forward. He had crossed his arms, but now he let them drop slowly, following the logic of the attack.
"You're targeting logistics. Not the center, but the periphery." He scratched his stubbled beard. "These are small targets, Commander. Once he realizes what we're preparing, he'll reinforce his defenses everywhere."
"He won't realize it." Rami turned to Akira. "The codes you extracted from Koriyama's systems before Kiryu... is secondary access still active?"
Akira pursed his lips, his fingers dancing across the console.
"We don't have full control. Koriyama updated the core cryptographic protocols with quantum encryption, but there are legacy monitoring subsystems that still accept our data packets." He looked up. "It will be enough for disinformation. I can inject phantom signals and fake signatures mimicking mobilization. On his sensors, it will look like a massive troop deployment."
"How many false targets can we simulate simultaneously?"
"With full synchronization across the electronic warfare platforms..." Akira calculated quickly. "Twenty. Maybe fifteen if we put an extra load on the Kage's reactors. It will look like a full-scale offensive on a dozen fronts."
Rami looked at the gathered officers. Beside Jin and Kenjiro stood Admiral Takeshi, who had returned two days ago from Kiryu, his face scarred by exhaustion. Next to him was Captain Nanako of the Fourteenth Destroyer Flotilla—a short, broad-shouldered woman with the gaze of someone used to making split-second decisions.
"Here is the plan," Rami announced.
Her voice remained quiet, but the room instantly fell silent. Everyone knew that when Rami spoke like this, her words carried the utmost weight.
"Akira will wreak havoc on Koriyama's intelligence through phantom attacks in a dozen locations. The enemy will scatter his forces in an attempt to defend everything. While his attention is diluted, we will strike the true targets swiftly and flawlessly."
She began to pace around the hologram, marking the sectors.
"The Fourteenth Flotilla takes Hara. Captain Nanako, I want a lightning raid. You get in, destroy the shipyard, and pull out. Maximum time in the system—forty minutes. Not a second more. The Seventh Tactical Group strikes Shibuya. You wreck the communications facilities. The Ninth Reserve attacks Yamada."
Nanako gave a curt nod.
With her arms crossed over her chest, Rami inadvertently betrayed her tension—the thumb of her left hand rapidly tapping her right elbow.
"And what will our main forces be doing?" Takeshi asked.
She looked at the old admiral. His face remained inscrutable. Takeshi didn't ask questions because he didn't know the answers; he did it to hear her formulate them in front of the others.
"They will regroup for the main objective."
The hologram transformed. The peripheral systems faded, and in the center of the projection remained a single point—crimson, blinking, surrounded by the concentric rings of fortifications, minefields, and orbital platforms. Ashikaga. Koriyama's capital. The heart of his power. A heavy sigh rippled through the room, but Rami ignored it.
"While Koriyama tries to decipher our maneuvers on the periphery, we will prepare the decisive blow. Every action we take from here on out is merely a step toward this moment."
The ensuing silence belonged to people accustomed to calculating odds, dangers, and costs. The officers around her had survived enough battles to grasp the magnitude of her proposal.
Takeshi spoke first. His voice was slow, every word measured.
"Your Highness, the plan is bold. It requires flawless synchronization between units scattered across several star systems. One lapse in coordination and the entire operation will collapse."
"That is true," Rami agreed.
"And Ashikaga," he continued, "is the most heavily fortified point in the sector. Orbital platforms, minefields, defense fleets. Even if we achieve surprise, the cost..."
He trailed off. He didn't need to finish. Everyone knew the casualties would be measured in numbers from Kiryu. Or worse.
Rami stepped toward him. Though she was a full head shorter, her physical height was the last thing that drew attention in the room.
"Admiral, how much longer can we sustain this war?"
Takeshi met her gaze. The wrinkles around his mouth deepened.
"With current losses and production rates—six months. Maybe eight if we fully mobilize the civilian sector. A year at most before our resources are completely depleted."
"And how long can Koriyama hold out on the defensive?"
The answer required no words. The longer the conflict dragged on, the more invulnerable the enemy became. Fortifications multiplied, and the industry in his central systems operated flawlessly. Time was Koriyama's ally.
"I understand," Takeshi nodded.
"I have no intention of sending our people into a reckless slaughter," Rami said. "On every ship we send into the fire stands someone's son. Someone's daughter. A father who promised to return." She turned to the map. "But sometimes the only way to save more lives is to stake everything on one critical moment. Not because it's safe, but because the alternative is ruinous."
Kenjiro stood off to the side, his right hand resting on the hilt of his katana. He listened in silence. This was not his moment, but hers. Yet Rami felt his gaze—as unwavering as an anchor in a stormy sea.
"I am offering you a strategy. We will work out the details together to ensure its operational effectiveness," she addressed the commanders. "The plan is realistic. It is neither easy nor certain, but it is a path to the end of this war. A true end. No truces, no demarcation lines, and no frozen conflicts that will flare up again in a decade. An end."
Nanako stood up first.
"The Fourteenth Flotilla is ready, Commander."
"Seventh Tactical Group as well," another officer added.
One by one they declared their readiness—tersely, professionally, without unnecessary pathos. Professionals who had made their decision. Rami valued exactly that.
Finally, only Takeshi remained. He didn't take his eyes off the crimson patch of Ashikaga. Rami noticed how his fingers behind his back clenched into a fist and relaxed again. Decades of experience whispered to him of the risks, while his intuition indicated that the woman before him was right.
"Your Highness," he murmured. "This is madness."
Rami looked him straight in the eyes.
"Yes."
"Magnificent madness. But perhaps that is exactly what we need right now."
He stood up and bowed—formally, strictly, with that weight of gesture that said more than any confession.
"The First Fleet is at your disposal."
 
- 2 -

Over the next few hours, the command center transformed. The map suspended above them updated with relentless dynamism—coordinates vanished and were born anew, timelines stretched and contracted, and blue and red symbols rearranged their positions until the plan took on a clear outline.
Three concentric circles formed around the tactical projection. On the outermost edge stood the staff officers and technicians, absorbed in calculations and transmitting orders over encrypted channels. In the middle—the fleet commanders, each focused on their own sector. At the very center, Rami, Kenjiro, Akira, Jin, and Takeshi remained hunched over the hologram like architects over a blueprint whose logic was about to dictate destinies.
Someone had brought tea and porcelain cups. Though out of place in this martial setting, they appeared and disappeared along the edges of the consoles. Rami's gaze lingered on one of them, and the memory of Captain Yamada surfaced instantly. She shoved the feeling aside immediately, forcing her thoughts back to the map.
Not now. Later. Always later.
She stood motionless in the heart of the hologram. The others moved around her—checking terminals, returning with new data, arguing in hushed tones. Rami was the core around which the plan crystallized.
"The timeframes," she interjected without raising her eyes. "We are going phase by phase. What is required for each of them?"
Akira stepped closer. His face looked paler than usual, a deep vertical crease cutting across his forehead. Ever since emerging from stasis, he had been working without rest, and his body was rebelling against the pace. The fingers of his left hand twitched slightly whenever they weren't touching a console. Rami kept silent. Akira knew his limits and would ask for a break when he needed one. Or Ishida would do it for him.
"Regarding the electronic warfare," he began, "I can activate the reconnaissance traffic right away. Low-intensity probes that won't trigger their defense algorithms. Standard perimeter sweeps. Koriyama's people will perceive it as background noise."
He initiated a simulation on the hologram. Fine blue lines sprouted around the red clusters—a web of phantom sensor signals that gradually thickened.
"After forty-eight hours, I'll increase the intensity. I'll add single-ship signatures in three sectors. Small, insignificant. Koriyama will assume they're recon missions and send out his own probes in response." The lines darkened and stretched. "On the seventh day, I'll add five more points. On the tenth—formations. After that, I'll simulate the massing of entire fleets. When the time comes for the real strike, he'll be monitoring fifteen targets simultaneously, and not a single one will be the real thing."
"Will that take seven days?" Rami asked.
"Seven to build the model convincingly." Akira looked at her, and in his eyes, she recognized a familiar severity. "Rami, if they figure out the pattern before the strike, we lose everything. Not just the element of surprise. Koriyama will know exactly what we're planning. And we won't get a second chance."
"I know."
"I wanted to be sure."
"I know, Akira."
She held his gaze a little longer. Not as a commander, but as a sister. For a fleeting moment, the memory of the stasis chamber passed between them, of the five weeks she had stood by him every night, speaking to a body that could not answer.
Akira gave a curt nod and returned to his console.
Kenjiro emerged quietly from the logistics sector. Rami sensed his presence by the distinct silence that always accompanied him.
"The ships won't last seven days," he said as soon as she turned around. In his right hand, he held a terminal displaying columns of numbers. His left arm, secured in a splint, hung motionless. "If our main forces wait at forward positions for a whole week, they'll attract attention. Koriyama has enough sensors to notice any massing of ships."
"Then don't concentrate them." Rami shrugged. "Disperse them. Does that solve the problem?"
Kenjiro nodded, as if he had expected that exact answer. He swiped to the next page on the screen.
"I am splitting the First Fleet into four groups. Each will maneuver independently in adjacent sectors. They'll be resupplied separately by civilian freighters—we'll hide the fuel and munitions in the cargo holds using forged manifest codes." He looked up. "Five routes outside the monitored corridors. Fourteen thousand tons a week."
"Will it be enough?"
"For a six-week operation—yes. Assuming proper resource allocation." He paused for a moment. "Ishida will insist on double the medical supplies."
"Give him triple."
Kenjiro accepted the order without surprise. He highlighted the number with a swift motion.
"Triple," he repeated quietly. Then he lowered his voice even further. "You are preparing for the worst."
"I am preparing for everything."
He did not reply.
He noted something else on his datapad, closed it, and walked away toward the logistics officers.
Rami stared into the empty space where he had stood only a moment ago.
"Triple medical reserves," she said aloud, before she even allowed herself to think it. Over the past few months, it had become a habit—articulating decisions before logic could catch up with them. Logic always followed intuition—slower and far more timid.
Above her, the hologram flickered. The map rearranged itself—Akira had initiated the first real-time simulation of the electronic warfare. Blue dots began multiplying around the red clusters, unfolding into nebulous swarms. Koriyama's sensors would detect them as a dense fog.
Takeshi approached. He had taken off his dress jacket; he stood in just a dark blue shirt bearing a faintly visible commander's insignia. The wrinkles around his eyes had carved themselves deeper over the last few hours. He looked toward the Ashikaga sector.
"There are thirty-two orbital platforms," he began immediately. "Minefields block the two main approach vectors. They have three fleets on constant standby and another two in reserve orbits." He sighed wearily. "Their defenses surpass those at Kiryu by fifty percent."
"What if we hit them outside the two main vectors?"
Takeshi looked at her. Something resembling approval flashed in his eyes. Not for the plan itself, but for the direction of her thoughts.
"The third possible vector runs through the hyper-gravitational belt. No one attacks from there because the gravitational gradients tear hulls apart." He activated his screen and began to trace lines. "But if we have the precise trajectories and pilots who have mastered their machines to perfection, we can punch through. I estimate the anticipated losses during the transit itself at five to seven percent. And that's before even engaging in combat."
"What is the alternative?"
"Taking the two main vectors. Breaching losses will be between twenty and thirty percent."
Rami turned to the hologram. The hyper-gravitational belt was depicted as a blurred dark band at the edge of the Ashikaga system—a zone of warped space-time, where the immense pull crushed any improperly positioned vessel. Her father had told her about it once, when she was still a child—stories of suicide pilots who had traversed it during the First System War.
No one attacks through the belt. Which is exactly why the enemy won't expect it.
"Prepare the trajectories," she ordered. "I want them by tomorrow morning. Along with a list of the pilots you believe can pull this off."
"It will be a short list," Takeshi noted.
"I know."
He nodded and walked away.
The hours bled into one another. Rami did not move from the center of the map. Around her, the command center breathed to its own rhythm—shifts changed silently, cups of coffee and tea appeared and vanished, and voices merged into a low, steady hum. The place operated with the cold precision of an automated mechanism. The hologram shifted with every decision—new routes surfaced, old ones faded, and timelines tightened like fingers curling into a fist.
She watched the plan materialize. She hadn't forged it alone—Akira had engineered the electronic warfare as a web of disinformation, Kenjiro had split the logistics into five invisible streams, Takeshi had pointed the way through the belt, and Nanako and her officers had charted the strike on Hara down to the second. Rami's job was simple: to see how all these pieces interlocked into a whole. And to bear the responsibility. No one had taught her how to carry such a burden.
She was learning on the fly.
When the details were finalized and the last objections exhausted, she turned to Jin. The admiral hadn't sat down once. His face was gray with exhaustion, but his gaze remained sharp.
"How much time is required for full mobilization?"
Jin ran a hand over his stubbled jaw and made a quick calculation.
"Seventy-two hours, minimum. Ninety-six to be certain."
"You have seventy."
Jin didn't argue. He didn't say it was impossible, nor did he ask for additional resources. He punched the number into his personal terminal with a sharp motion, turned, and strode toward the comms officers.
Rami was left alone in the center of the map.
Above her, the galaxy continued its slow rotation—stars, worlds, and fleets moving along predestined trajectories. Seventy hours until the start of the electronic diversion. Thirteen days until the first strikes on Koriyama's periphery. Then—the hyper-gravitational belt. After that—Ashikaga.
And finally—the end. One way or another.
She gripped the edge of the console. Her hands turned white for a moment before she let go.
"Commander," a quiet voice spoke behind her.
Kenjiro. He had returned unnoticed. In his right hand, he held a small porcelain cup of tea. He offered it to her silently.
She took the cup. The tea was hot, and the steam rose in a thin thread, dissolving into the bluish light of the hologram.
"Thank you," she said.
Kenjiro didn't answer. He remained beside her, staring at the red dot of Ashikaga. They stood like that for a long time—the two of them, the cup, the map above them, and the silence that needed no explanation.
