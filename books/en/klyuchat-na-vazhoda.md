Chapter 1: The Dogs

The dog had him by the left arm and was pulling.
Lucius opened his eyes among wet leaves. A gray muzzle was at his elbow, and his own skin was stretched taut between its teeth. The beast was hauling backward with all its weight, hunched low, belly to the leaves, growling. The pain came a moment later, along with the cold, and only then did he realize he was naked.
He kicked with all his strength. His heel sank into something soft. The dog yelped and let go. Lucius rolled through the beech litter, dry leaves clinging to his back and thighs, and pushed himself up onto one knee.
Dusk was already falling.
The dog was not alone. Three more slipped out from between the trunks—lean, their ribs jutting beneath matted fur, their muzzles held low to the ground. A fifth appeared behind them and sat down on its haunches to wait for the others to finish off the prey.
Lucius reached for his name.
He wanted it before he fought, but his groping found nothing. Where he searched for it there was only a smooth, warm hollow—like a bed someone had just risen from. He did not remember how he had come to this forest, whose it was, or how long he had been lying in it. The leaves beneath his back were dry, but the ones heaped over him were wet. So he had been lying here for hours at least.
The gray dog sprang.
Lucius threw his arm up in front of his throat—he had nothing else to defend himself with.
The teeth sank into his left arm again, higher this time, into the flesh below the elbow. The beast’s weight knocked him flat on his back. His breath left him in a hiss.
Above his face a muzzle snarled, dripping saliva. The stench of carrion and wet fur flooded his nostrils. His right hand groped blindly through the leaves for a stone, a root—anything at all.
It found a thick, dry oak branch, heavier at one end.
He struck with it once, blindly. The blow landed on the dog’s shoulder. He struck a second time and hit the head.
The dog let go.
Lucius was on his feet before he had decided to stand. His body knew things he himself did not remember: where to step so as not to slip on the leaves, how to grip the branch with both hands even though the left was bleeding, how to move so that the thick beech stayed at his back. His hands were young, with long, slender fingers, yet the branch lay in them like the haft of a weapon carried for years.
The other three dogs, growling low, fanned out in a half circle.
They were in no hurry, and that was worse than the lunge. They kept trading places. One would edge forward while another drew back, and all their eyes were fixed on the arm that dripped blood.
The fifth, the one that had been sitting until now, was already on its feet.
The first came from the left, low, at his legs. Lucius met it with the heavy end of the branch square in the muzzle. The jaw cracked.
The second came in from the right at the same instant and bit him above the knee. Lucius cried out, struck downward, and missed. He struck again. The branch glanced off the dog’s skull and came down on its back, and the beast twisted and let go.
Blood ran down Lucius’s leg—warm at first, then colder and colder.
The gray one had come back. It stopped three paces in front of him, head lowered, and the others fell into line behind it on their own. They were panting through open mouths, and so was Lucius. Between them, steam rose into the air.
That was when he spoke.
“Back.”
The voice was not loud. It came out of his chest, level and cold, the voice of a man ordering servants to clear away the dishes. Lucius heard it with surprise—he had not known he possessed such a voice.
The dogs stopped. The gray one flattened its ears against its skull. One of those at the back retreated a step and crouched, and another turned its head away.
It lasted as long as it took to draw a breath.
Then hunger won out. The gray one bared its teeth and stepped forward, and the others followed.
Anger flooded Lucius, hot, stronger than fear and stronger than pain. The wounds in his arm and above his knee were not half as galling as the fact that five starving curs had understood in an instant who stood before them and had chosen to disregard him.
“Back, I said!”
This time they did not stop.
The gray one lunged for his throat. But this time Lucius did not raise his arm to shield himself. Instead he swung the branch upward with his whole body, from his heels through his hip to his shoulders. The heavy end met the dog’s jaw while it was still in midair. The impact rang through his palms.
The beast flipped over, landed on its side in the leaves, kicked its legs, and went still.
Lucius did not give it a second glance. He turned toward the others and advanced on them, naked, the branch raised over his head. A sound tore from his throat that was not a word.
The first dog backed away. The second turned and fled low to the ground, tail tucked between its legs, and the rest bolted after it among the trunks.
The rustling in the leaves receded and died away. Only his heavy breathing remained.
He stood over the dead dog, shaking.
Fear had a share in it, but the cold was stronger. A little while ago he had not felt it at all, but now it pressed in on him from every side. His teeth chattered. He clenched them, but the shivering passed into his jaw and would not stop. Above him the beeches let their leaves go one by one through the still air, and every one that touched him was cold. The light on the treetops was already fading.
He crouched beside the dog. It was smaller than it had seemed in the heat of the fight—nothing but skin and bone, its flanks caved in. Around its neck was a worn leather collar, almost hidden in its matted fur. Once it had guarded someone’s yard and eaten from someone’s bowl.
Lucius looked at the collar for a while, then at his arm.
The left arm was torn in two places, below the elbow and in the middle of the forearm. From the lower wound hung a flap of skin the size of a thumb. The blood did not gush, but it flowed steadily in thin trickles that ran down between his fingers. The wound above his knee was lighter. He pressed the arm to his chest, and his pulse began to pound in the wound.
He had to bind it, and he had nothing to bind it with. Nor anything to cover himself with. He was naked in a forest he did not know, the light was going, and the only thing he owned was the oak branch.
Lucius gripped it and stood. If he stayed beside the dead dog, he would be taking a chance.
He set off downhill, because downhill was easier.
The slope dipped toward a low place from which came the sound of water. Lucius walked toward the sound, leaning on the branch, his left arm pressed to his chest. With every step his knee stabbed him. The roots under the leaves were slick, and he fell twice. The second time he came down on his wounded arm and lay facedown in the leaves until the pain ebbed enough for him to see.
The stream turned out to be narrow and swift, its stones furred with moss. Lucius knelt on the bank and drank, scooping water with his right hand. It was so cold that it made his jaw ache and his throat go numb. He bent lower, though he could not have said why, and looked into the still water behind a stone.
The light was almost gone, but a face took shape in the water—pale, with dark hair plastered to the forehead and dark eyes under straight brows. The face was young, the cheeks smooth, the beard only just beginning to show on them.
Lucius stared at it until the water broke it apart, unable to say what exactly troubled him about it.
At last he understood. He had expected something else—something older.
He had no memory of another face, only the expectation, and it did not leave him no matter how long he looked.
Teeth clenched, he carefully washed his wounded arm. He tore a handful of moss from a stone and pressed it into the wound.
The moss stung, but there was nothing else he could do. He rose and went on.
The darkness was already thick when he smelled the smoke. At first he did not believe it. The smell was so faint that he took it for a memory. But then the wind shifted along the course of the stream and brought the smoke again, thicker. And with it came another smell, bitter and warm, like grain left to scorch in the embers. His mouth flooded with saliva. He did not know the smell, but wherever it smelled like that, there was a fire, and people roasting something.
He followed the stream. He walked faster, though he could not see where he was stepping. He did not fall once, and he thought of nothing but the fire.
Soon a red light began to flicker between the trunks, swaying across the low branches. Voices reached him. A woman was singing without words, and a man’s voice answered her.
The forest ended abruptly at the edge of a clearing. In the middle of it two fires burned, ringed by tall wagons with roofs of stretched canvas. People moved about the camp, and beside the wagons horses grazed quietly, tethered to stakes driven into the ground. A cauldron hung over one of the fires, and children chased one another around it. The bitter smell came from there. A low mist drifted over the clearing, mingled with smoke and the steam of the horses’ breath.
Lucius stopped behind the last beech and stayed there.
From here the camp looked larger: ten or so wagons, perhaps more, for the ring disappeared behind the fire. And people—mostly silhouettes in the dark, too many to count. The men wore wide sashes, the women shawls thrown over their shoulders. Everyone talked loudly, over one another, with no fear of being heard.
One woman was stirring the cauldron. Another was pouring something dark from a pan into a copper cup, and the bitter smell rose every time she swirled the pan. By the fire an old man was slicing bread, the loaf pressed against his chest, and handing the pieces back over his shoulder without looking to see who took them.
Lucius’s body wanted to go to the bread at once. He held it back.
He could circle around. He could crawl along the wagons on the dark side to the horses, where horse blankets hung from a rope, and filch one before anyone noticed. He could wait for the people to fall asleep and approach the fire only when nothing but embers remained. He weighed it all soberly, move by move, and every move was sensible.
But every move was the move of a man who skulks.
He watched the light on the wagon canvas and could not picture himself crawling toward it on his belly. He did not remember what he had been, but of one thing he was sure: he did not come in through the back door. He came in where he could be seen, and waited for them to make way.
The decision was foolish—he was naked and wounded, and there were many of them.
He pushed off from the beech and stepped out of the forest.
Beneath one of the wagons a dog began to bark.
The song broke off. The people around the fire turned. Someone shouted something he could not make out, and the children vanished behind the wall of wagons. Two young men snatched thick poles up from the ground.
Lucius did not stop. He walked toward the fire slowly—he could go no faster. Naked, bloodied, leaning on the branch.
Only when he reached the gap between two wagons did a man step out of the light and plant himself in front of him.
“Stay right there.”
The man was broad in the shoulders, shorter than Lucius, with a close-cropped beard shot with gray. In his hand he held a short, broad knife, the kind used for skinning game. He held it low by his thigh, blade pointing down. Behind him the others were gathering: women in shawls, an old man, the two youths with their poles.
Lucius stopped. He could not have gone on anyway.
The man looked him over slowly from head to toe, and his mouth twisted. His gaze lingered on the arm with the moss, on the bloody knee, then on the branch.
“Well now, what hole did you crawl out of?”
“The forest.” Lucius’s voice came out hoarse, and he cleared his throat. “There were dogs.”
“I can see there were dogs.” The man did not lower the knife. “Drop the stick.”
Lucius did not drop it. The branch was the only thing he had, and he was not about to throw it down in the grass in front of a man with a knife. He planted the heavy end on the ground and leaned on it.
“I need a fire,” he said. “And something to bind my arm.”
“Need, do you?” The man gave a short laugh, but his eyes stayed cold. “Hear that, Yasmina? He needs a fire, he says.”
“I heard, Raul.” The woman at his shoulder was wiry, with black braids streaked with white, her arms folded beneath her shawl. She was looking at Lucius the way one looks at a snake on the doorstep. “I heard, and I don’t like it.”
“Listen, boy.” Raul took a step forward. “This fire is for our own. We don’t want strangers. Go back to the stream and follow it down. By morning, if you haven’t died by then, you’ll come out on the road.”
The cold shook him from within. Lucius clenched his teeth so it wouldn’t show. If he started shivering in front of this man, it would look as though he were begging. He lifted his chin and, half a head taller than Raul, looked down at him—naked and bloodied, moss in his wound. He knew he looked absurd, but he also knew that no one had asked him whether he wanted to leave.
“I’m not going to die,” he said. “Nor am I going to follow the stream. I’ll sit by your fire and get warm, and then I’ll decide where to go.”
“You’ll decide!” Raul repeated slowly, word by word. “Did you all hear that? He’s going to decide.”
No one laughed. One of the young men took his pole in both hands.
From the back of the crowd came the voice of the old man with the bread. He was still holding the knife and the loaf, and he was watching Lucius over the others’ heads.
“Naked, out of the forest,” he said quietly, but everyone heard him. “Like that other one.”
Raul did not turn, but his shoulders tightened.
“Hold your tongue, Petre.”
“I’m only saying he’s like that other one.”
“I know what you’re saying.” Raul did not take his eyes off Lucius. “We all know.”
Lucius did not know who that other one was, and he did not ask. But the old man’s words had changed something in the faces around him. The women, who until then had been watching him with curiosity, lowered their eyes. Yasmina laid a hand on Raul’s shoulder, and it was hard to tell whether she was holding him back or leaning on him.
Raul was silent so long that the fire behind him crackled twice.
When he spoke again, his voice was quieter and cold, and something more dangerous had crept into it:
“I’ll tell you how these things go, boy. Someone comes out of the forest, naked and bloodied, and we all take pity on him. We give him a shirt, we give him a place by the fire. And then others come—clothed ones. And then nobody takes pity on us.”
Lucius stepped forward, into the light.
His knee buckled on the very first step, and he had to throw all his weight onto the branch.
Raul raised the knife to the level of his belly.
“One more step and I’ll send you after the dogs.”
Lucius watched the knife. He knew he was weak, that the loss of blood was cutting his legs out from under him, and that the man facing him was well fed and rested. If it came to a fight, he would strike upward with the branch, at the wrist, and then drive his shoulder into the man’s chest. He would not succeed, but he would not back away either.
“Raul.”
The voice came from one side—quiet, unforced. The circle parted reluctantly.
A woman made her way through the crowd. First came the glint of silver rings on her hands, then the black hair hanging loose down her back, and at last the dark skirts and the shawl. In the firelight her eyes looked almost yellow, but when she stepped between Raul and Lucius and turned to face him, he saw that they were green.
She looked at him for a long time. She studied his face and the wound with the moss, then met his eyes again. A shadow crossed her face and was gone.
Only then did she turn to Raul and lay her palm over the hand that held the knife.
“Not him.”
 
Chapter 2: The Palm

The horse blanket smelled of horse and prickled against his bare shoulders, but it was warm. Lucius held it closed under his chin with his right hand while the woman with the green eyes cleaned the left.
He sat on a wooden stump by the fire, so close that his knees burned. The woman knelt before him in the grass. She had already pulled the moss from the wound without asking, and now she was pouring something from a clay flask over the torn flesh. The liquid smelled of pears, and it stung.
Lucius stared into the fire and made no sound.
She glanced up at him, then lowered her eyes again. Her lips twitched.
“My name is Ayshe,” she said. “You can scream if you want. No one here will think you weaker for it.”
“I don’t want to.”
“So I see.”
She took a needle already threaded with horsehair and held it in the flames for a moment. She closed the lower wound with five stitches. Her fingers were dry and quick.
Lucius watched the needle go into his skin and come out again. Turning his head away seemed worse to him than the pain.
When she was done, she covered the stitches with a dark, thick salve that smelled of pine resin and bound his arm with strips of linen—snug, but not so tight that it hurt.
On the other side of the fire, Raul and Yasmina were arguing. They did not lower their voices—they wanted him to hear.
“So you’ll keep him by the fire, and then what?” Raul stood with his fists planted on his hips. “Then you’ll put him to bed in your wagon, won’t you? And tomorrow you’ll give him a horse.”
“Tomorrow he leaves.” Ayshe did not look up from the bandage.
“That other one was going to leave tomorrow too.” Yasmina was leaning forward, elbows on her knees, her shawl hanging almost into the fire. “For weeks on end, it was always tomorrow he’d be leaving.”
Ayshe tied the last knot and only then turned to them.
“I know what happened with that other one. I was there.”
“We were all there.” Raul stepped toward the fire. The flames lit his face from below—heavy and broad, the face of a man used to carrying loads. His jaw was clenched. “It’s been twelve years, Ayshe. You took him in, and then the knights of Azur came for us, overturned our wagons, and beat Petre in front of the children.”
Lucius looked over at Petre, the old man with the bread. He sat a little apart, head bowed, still cutting slices from the loaf. His left eye, white as a boiled egg, stared into the fire.
“We couldn’t stop them then, and we won’t be able to now,” Yasmina said. “Send him on his way before they come again.”
Ayshe rose to her feet. Raul stood a full head taller than she did, but the moment she planted herself before the fire, the two across from her fell silent before she had even opened her mouth.
“Last time the fault was mine. And this time, if there’s any fault, it’ll be mine again. Tonight he stays here. In the morning he goes.”
Raul gave her a long look, then spat into the fire and stalked off toward the horses. Yasmina stayed where she was, but she turned her face away.
Lucius sat with the blanket clutched under his chin. No one had asked him whether he would leave in the morning—the woman had said it in passing, between two knots. He wanted to tell her that he would decide for himself when to go and where. But the fire was warm, the bandage held his arm as firmly as a hand, and he kept quiet, angrier with himself than with her.
Later Ayshe brought him clothes—a shirt of coarse cloth, woolen trousers, a sheepskin vest with the fleece turned inward, and soft shoes that laced with thongs around the ankle. Everything was worn, but clean.
“Whose are these?” he asked.
“A boy’s who used to live with us.” She set them on the stump beside him. “They don’t fit him anymore. He grew.”
He dressed behind the wagon—slowly, because of his arm. The shirt was short in the sleeves and loose across the chest, and the vest smelled of a stranger.
When he came back to the fire, someone had left a bowl of stew and a hunk of bread on the stump. He ate with his head bowed. Only when he had wiped the bowl clean with the last crust did he realize how hungry he had been.
Raul came back from the horses with a steaming copper cup. The bitter smell Lucius had caught back in the forest reached him before the cup did.
“Drink. It’s zharka.” Raul held the cup out to him. “The kernels are roasted over embers, then boiled. Warms you better than wine.”
Lucius looked at the cup, then at the hand that held it. Less than an hour ago the same hand had held a knife to his belly. If he took the cup, he would be taking it from a man who wanted him dead, or at least far away from here. And that man would know Lucius owed him.
His throat was parched.
“I’m not thirsty.”
Raul looked him up and down, shrugged, and drank it himself.
“Suit yourself. Nobody’s going to beg you.”
He walked off with the cup.
A little later a boy of seven or eight came running up to the stump, with black eyes that took up half his face. He held out a wooden dipper of water from the barrel, and Lucius drained it in one gulp. The boy stayed. He sat down on the ground, hugged his knees, and stared at him.
“What town are you from?” he asked.
“I don’t know.”
“Then where’s your horse?”
“I don’t have a horse.”
“We have twelve.” The boy jerked his chin toward the darkness, where snorting could be heard. “And not one of them is shod. Uncle Raul says you can hear a shod horse three meadows away, and we only want people to hear us when we sing.”
“Deno, go to sleep!” Yasmina called from across the fire.
The boy sprang up and ran off into the dark.
Later still there was singing, though not everyone joined in. The men had drifted off to the wagons, and only the women, Petre, and two young men whittling something with their knives remained by the fire. In the end Yasmina sang alone, softly, for herself, and the others fell silent.
The song was old—everyone knew when the next verse would come. The melody went around in a circle and kept coming back to the same place, like a horse on a tether.
Azur had seven sons, seven shining stars,

and Lucifer, the brightest, walked ahead of all.

He wanted his father’s throne, he wanted heaven for his own,

and Azur in his wrath sent the thunder down.

Thunder struck from heaven, and the seven fell,

and where they fell, the grass would grow no more.
There were more verses—about where each of them had fallen, about the forest that burned beneath one and the sea that drew back before another. To Lucius it was a tale for other people’s children. But the song was beautiful, Yasmina’s voice was low and husky, and the fire was warm. His shoulders, tense since the moment he had woken in the leaves, finally loosened.
When the song ended, no one said anything. Petre nodded to himself.
“Do you believe that?” Lucius asked.
Yasmina looked at him across the fire. Until now she had not spoken to him directly. Up close she looked younger than he had thought—the lines around her eyes came from the sun and wind of the roads.
“I sing the songs because they earn us bread. We Vestari carry the news from kingdom to kingdom, all across Estera, and wherever we stop, we pay with a song or with tidings. People love the one about the Fallen. They weep over it, and then they give us a bigger loaf.” She smiled crookedly. “If I believed the songs, I’d never get any sleep.”
“Why not?”
“Because they say the Fallen come back.” Yasmina stood and brushed off her skirts. “Good night, stranger.”
The fire had dwindled to embers when Ayshe came and sat down beside him. For a while she said nothing, gazing into the glow. Then she reached with bare fingers to the edge of the hearth and scooped up a handful of ash—cool on top, still warm underneath.
“Give me your hand. The right one.”
“What for?”
“You want to know who you are, don’t you?” She did not look at him. “Or are you going to wait for it to come to you on its own?”
Lucius held out his hand.
Ayshe turned it palm up and sprinkled it with ash. She rubbed it in slowly with her fingertips until the gray had worked into every crease. The lines of his palm stood out dark, like paths drawn in charcoal. She studied them for a long time, tilting her head first to one side, then to the other, and said nothing.
She set her thumb in the center of his palm.
That was where the lines met. She pressed—lightly at first, then harder, until the pressure reached his wrist and spread up his arm. Her other fingers curled around the back of his hand and held it steady.
Her thumb did not move. Ayshe lifted her eyes from his hand to his face and studied him from close up, looking straight into his eyes. In the glow of the embers, her green eyes had darkened.
“So much pride,” she said.
Lucius wanted to pull his hand away. He did not—something in the way she pressed with her thumb held him there.
“Is that what you see in the ash?”
“In the ash I see that you have fallen.”
She let go of his hand.
In the middle of his palm her thumb had left a circle clean of ash, and the skin there was white.
“Fallen from where?” Lucius leaned toward her. “From a horse? From a cliff? From someone’s good graces?”
Ayshe wiped her fingers on her skirt and did not answer.
“Tell me.”
He had not meant it as a command, but that was how it came out—in the same cold voice he had heard inside himself in the forest, when the dogs stopped.
Ayshe raised her eyes. For an instant something watchful flickered in them, something he could not make sense of—like someone who hears footsteps in the next room and knows whose they are. Then she only laughed softly.
“I’m not your servant girl, boy.”
“I never said you were.”
“Your voice said it.” She leaned back against the wagon wheel. “What’s your name?”
Lucius opened his mouth. Again he reached for that smooth place inside himself where his name should have been. Again he found it empty.
He closed his mouth.
“So you don’t know yet.” There was no mockery in Ayshe’s voice. “That’s all right. Not everyone has to know everything at once.”
“And do you know it?”
“I know a great many things I have no use for.” She looked toward the embers. “Now listen, because I’ll only say this once. Two days’ journey from here, if you take the straight path, lies Altara. It’s a big city, built on a hill. Our kingdom is called Superbia, and Altara is its highest city, so it thinks itself the most important. There’s a seer there. Her name is Cassandra.”
“And what does she see?”
“She sees what I can only smell.” Ayshe stretched her fingers over the embers to warm them. “If anyone can tell you where you fell from, she can.”
“Where do I look for her?”
“Look in the lower streets. Down there everyone knows who holds what.” She paused. “You may not find her easily.”
