Chapter 1: The Wine

The wine in Matthias’s glass was worth more than the life of the man who had tended the vine.
Ansgar had said so himself, while he was still pouring, and now he was waiting to hear it said back to him. He leaned over the table, both hands planted on the cloth. Grease from the roast glistened on his chin.
“The vineyards burned in the war for the southern coast,” he said. “Forty years ago. There are three casks of that vintage left in all of Gula, and one of them is mine. Seven years I’ve kept this bottle for an occasion.”
“And the occasion is me.”
Ansgar laughed too loudly and too soon. It was how people laughed when they couldn’t tell whether Matthias was joking. Matthias rarely joked. He said what he thought in the voice other men saved for witticisms, and left the listener to decide for himself what he had heard.
He raised the glass to the candles and swirled it. The wine slid down the inside in dark, slow trails. He did not drink.
The table was Ansgar’s, and so was the house, but each of the twelve guests glanced at Matthias whenever he thought no one was watching. Ansgar wanted a share in the grain ships Matthias sent north every autumn, and he was ready to pledge half his estate for it. The other half Matthias already held. He had bought it up through three straw creditors and a promissory note Ansgar had signed before a moneychanger in Satura, the capital. Ansgar still believed the note had stayed there. By spring Matthias would have the rest as well.
“Have you given any thought to the ships?” Ansgar asked. He was no longer laughing. “I’m offering three thousand for a quarter share. I can go higher, if you let me into the northern ports.”
“May I ask where the three thousand comes from?”
“From my salt warehouse. From the house in Satura.” Ansgar spread his arms over the table. “From everything you see.”
Matthias looked the table over: the roast, the silver platters, the pearls at the throat of Ansgar’s wife. Half of it had been his since summer.
“I’ll think on it until spring.”
“Until spring!”
“Grain doesn’t sail in winter, Ansgar. There’s no reason to hurry.”
Ansgar nodded, pleased with the delay, and did not understand what he had heard.
Ansgar was easy. He had always been easy, and of late that had begun to weigh on Matthias.
Gula, the kingdom of feasts, laid its wealth on the table for all to see. Matthias had lived in Merante, its port, for thirty years. He had traded with six of the seven kingdoms of Estera, and nowhere else did the rich eat so gladly in front of the hungry. Beneath the chandeliers the table blazed with gold and silver: the platters, the embroidery on the men’s doublets, the rings on the women’s fingers, the saltcellar of gilded silver shaped like a ship under full sail.
The flame of the nearest candle bowed flat and rose again. Light ran across the gilding of the saltcellar, and for an instant Matthias saw no saltcellar there at all, only gold.
The gold was calling to him. The call came from beneath his breastbone, from a place Matthias guarded from any touch, the way the tongue keeps clear of a broken tooth.
Take it. It is yours. Everything has always been yours.
Warmth ran through his fingers, thick and slow, and crept up to his wrists. His hand reached for the saltcellar of its own accord. Matthias drew it back into his lap.
He closed his eyes and counted to seven. Then he opened them.
The warmth ebbed, reluctantly. No one at the table had noticed a thing. Matthias had long ago learned to hide the call behind a smile and a glance turned elsewhere, but over these past months it had come more often and left more slowly. He hated not knowing what a thing was worth. What this was worth, he did not know. He knew only that it was growing.
“Have you heard who’s come ashore in Merante?” Ansgar leaned closer still, glad to have something to offer his guest. “Gernot, cupbearer to Prince Cedric, Oswin’s younger brother. The one who was pouring at the feast in Satura when Oswin fell.”
Two of the guests stopped chewing. All of Gula had heard how Prince Oswin had fallen at the feast before his father’s eyes and how he now wore a monk’s habit. Everyone had a different tale of what had been in his cup.
Matthias set his own glass down on the cloth.
“He came ashore with six casks,” Ansgar went on. “Under Cedric’s seal, red wax on every bung. They say it’s an installment on the crown’s old debt.” He paused to watch Matthias’s face. “Owed to you, isn’t it? The whole quay knows.”
“The quay knows a great many things, Ansgar. Half of them I told it myself.”
Ansgar laughed again. Then he lowered his voice, pleased with what he had saved for last.
“Only the cupbearer isn’t selling wine. Twice he’s been to the moneychangers on the lower quay, asking who in Merante pays best. Not for the casks. For something he carries in his head.”
Under the table Matthias’s fingers curled and went slack.
Six years ago King Theodor had borrowed from him to build his fleet and had paid the installments in wine from the palace cellars. This autumn Theodor had died, and the debt had not. Now a servant of the court was asking the moneychangers what his knowledge was worth. Whoever sat on the throne of Gula would want both the servant and his knowledge back.
“Excuse me,” Matthias said, and rose.
The talk around the table died for a moment, and the guests pretended they had not been following him with their eyes. Ansgar made to get up.
“Sit.” Matthias raised a palm without turning. “The evening is yours. I only want a breath of air.”
The evening was not Ansgar’s. Matthias was letting him keep it one hour more.
The terrace looked out over the harbor. Below it the roofs of the lower town stepped down to the water, and beyond them the ships’ lanterns rocked on the black swell. Matthias counted them out of habit: twenty-three. The wind carried salt, tar, and the smell of fish left lying on the quay since morning. He leaned on the balustrade. The stone was cold under his palms. There was no one here to smile at, and he let his face go slack.
Once he had had a home. He did not remember it. All that remained of the memory was a hollow, like the impression of a seal lifted from the wax before the wax had cooled. Matthias filled it with everything he touched, and it never filled.
“A fine view,” said a voice behind him. “Especially if you like counting other people’s money.”
Matthias did not flinch. He turned slowly.
The woman stood by the column, where the light from the hall did not reach. Her hands showed first, bare, without a single ring, resting loosely one in the other. Then the lanterns below picked out her cheekbones and her hair, pinned tight at the nape. She wore dark, close-fitting clothes, with no jewelry and no crest. She had placed herself so that she could see the door to the hall and the stairs to the garden at the same time.
“The doors downstairs were locked,” Matthias said.
“Doors are always locked.” She shrugged. “Otherwise nobody would hire me.”
“I sent word to two go-betweens that I was looking for hands for quiet work. Neither of them mentioned you.”
“Your go-betweens take a tenth of the fee.” She stepped up to the balustrade, two paces from him, and stopped there. “I decided to spare you that. And myself.”
“You also knew which terrace I’d be on.”
“Ansgar told his cook, and the cook told the fisherman who brings him mussels. In Merante every servant sells his master’s suppers.” She looked at him. “Yours too, only dearer.”
He liked that. Matthias tucked it away and didn’t press her on how she knew.
“What’s your name?”
“Isabella.”
He did not give her his own. She knew it.
“Two days’ journey east of here there’s a monastery,” Matthias said. “Its hill is white with lichen, and that’s why they call it Silver Hill. In the monastery archive an old man named Lazar keeps the book of donations. In it there’s an entry for an object the monks recorded as ‘a silver key without a lock.’ I want that entry. All of it, exactly as written, with everything around it.”
“Not the object?”
“Not the object. Not yet.”
“A key without a lock.” She tilted her head. “What does it open?”
“If I knew that, I wouldn’t need the entry.”
“You’re lying.”
“I’m lying to you by half. The other half I’ll pay you for.”
“So. A line from a monks’ book.” Isabella repeated the words slowly, one at a time. “How much?”
“Twenty silver now. Twenty more when the entry is in my hands.”
“A hundred.”
“You don’t know yet what I’ll be paying you for.”
“I know what you’re telling me.” She leaned a hip against the balustrade. “Every rich man I’ve ever robbed tells me the thing is cheap, so he can pay me less. You tell me it’s a line from a book, and then you walk out of Ansgar’s feast to haggle with a thief in the dark. So the line is worth more than a hundred.”
Down on the quay someone struck a ship’s stern bell. The sound reached the terrace late and faint.
“Everyone wants something,” Matthias said. “The only difference between me and the rest is that I know the exact price.”
He took a purse from his belt and held it on his open palm, between them.
“Seventy. Half now.”
Isabella did not reach for the purse, and Matthias liked her for that.
“Eighty,” she said. “And if there’s someone else on that hill who wants the same thing, I’m under no obligation to die for your line.”
“If there’s someone else on that hill, you’ll tell me who. That’s what the eighty is for.”
She held his gaze a long while. Then she nodded, and only then did she take the purse. Her hand passed over his palm without touching it, and the purse vanished somewhere into the folds of her clothes before Matthias could see where.
“I’ll come and take the entry from your hands myself,” he said. “I don’t want it traveling.”
“So you don’t trust me.”
“So I’m paying you eighty instead of two hundred.”
She gave a short laugh through her nose and went down the stairs to the garden. Her steps made no sound on the gravel.
Matthias stayed at the balustrade. The twenty-three lanterns rocked below. Somewhere among them, in his warehouse on the lower quay, sat a man who was selling something that wasn’t wine.
He did not go back to the hall. He told the servant at the door to let Ansgar know that his guest was grateful and weary, and went down into the garden.
Bran was waiting below the terrace, in the shadow of the fig tree by the little gate to the street. Matthias had left him there before supper, and there he found him after every feast in Merante: a big man with a broad, rough-hewn face, his back to the wall and a pewter flask at his belt. Matthias had taken him on years ago, along with the debts of a nobleman from Satura. The nobleman had cut out Bran’s tongue, and ever since, Bran had spoken with his fingers. Later Matthias ruined the nobleman, and Bran stayed with him.
Now he pointed two fingers at the gravel of the garden, then touched the back of his head, where Isabella had pinned up her hair.
“I know,” Matthias said. “I hired her.”
Bran unhooked the flask and held it out. Matthias shook his head. Bran took a single swallow, just enough to wet his mouth, and hooked it back in place.
They went down toward the harbor. Bran walked half a step behind and to the left, on the side where Matthias carried no purse. For so large a man, he trod softly.
The warehouse smelled of wet wood and pitch. The watchman unlocked it and lifted his lantern higher than he needed to, to show he was awake. The six casks stood in a row along the wall, on wooden chocks. Every bung was sealed with red wax, and in the wax was the imprint of Cedric’s ring.
In front of the casks, on an upturned crate, sat a thin man with graying hair carefully combed to a part. When he saw who had come in, he stood. His hands stayed in his sleeves, though the warehouse was warm.
“You’re Matthias.”
“These casks are mine,” Matthias said. “The king of Gula owes them to me. And you’ve been sitting with them for three days without handing them over to my clerk.”
“The seal is His Highness’s. I didn’t set it, and I won’t be the one to break it.”
“I haven’t asked you to break it.”
Gernot glanced at the watchman. Matthias nodded, and the watchman set his lantern on a crate and went out. The door closed behind him.
Bran stayed on the inside of the door. Gernot’s eyes came to rest on him too.
“He doesn’t speak,” Matthias said.
Gernot took in Bran’s broad face, then his hands. He nodded, unconvinced.
“Ansgar says you’re selling something that isn’t wine.”
“Ansgar talks a great deal.”
“That’s why I go to his suppers.”
Gernot drew his hands out of his sleeves. They were pale and thin-fingered, and you could tell they had worn gloves all their lives. He looked at them as if they belonged to someone else, and tucked them away again.
“I want a ship south, out of Gula,” he said. “I want silver enough for a year. And I want protection from someone Cedric can’t buy.”
“Cedric can buy anyone, once he’s king.”
“Not you. He owes you.”
“Why are you here and not in Satura?”
“His Highness sent me off with the casks the day after the king’s funeral. He told me not to come back until the creditor had signed for them.” Gernot gave a crooked smile. “The creditor is you.”
“Then I’ll give you my signature and you’ll go back.”
“Then you’ll give me your signature, and someone with a knife will be waiting for me on the quay.” Gernot shook his head. “Cedric doesn’t send his cupbearer to cart casks around. He sent me here so I’d disappear, far from court. As long as I sit by the seal, I’m the king’s man, and nobody dares lay a hand on me in front of witnesses. The moment I get up, I’m nobody.”
The man reckoned like a merchant. Matthias very nearly liked him.
He sat down on one of the crates across from him. He was in no hurry. A man who had gone to the moneychangers twice would not leave the warehouse until he had sold.
“Say it,” he said. “If it’s true, I’ll pay you more than you’re asking. If it isn’t, I’ll send you back to Cedric along with the casks.”
Gernot licked his lips. He looked at the door, then at the casks, then at Matthias.
“It wasn’t the wine that felled Oswin. I poured the dew into his cup. With this hand.” He raised his right hand, still in its sleeve. “From a vial no bigger than a thumb, held low by the rim while I poured. Cedric gave me the order the night before. He told me how many drops and at which toast.” His voice sank. “Before the feast I went to King Theodor. I stood by his bed and asked him whether I should carry the ewer for Cedric. I told him which hand I’d be holding it in.”
“And the king?”
“The king was eating quail. He chewed until I left.” Gernot swallowed. “He didn’t say no. I waited for him to say no, and he just kept chewing.”
Bran had not stirred from the door, and while Gernot spoke he watched his mouth, as he watched the mouth of anyone who spoke in front of him. Matthias had long put it down to a mute man’s habit.
Matthias knew about the dew. The Cult of the Spear brewed it in a monastery up in the mountains: dark red, sweet, and thick as honey. Whoever drank it grew meek and craved more. Half of Satura whispered that Theodor had been giving it to his elder son to keep him obedient. No one had whispered that the king had known about the cup.
The cold beneath his breastbone, which always came after the call, rose and filled his chest. Matthias let it come. Beneath it his thoughts turned slow and clear.
Cedric would be king within the month, if he wasn’t already. The new king of Gula had poisoned his brother, and the old king had known and held his tongue. The man who could say so before the court was worth more than six casks and more than the debt. For that truth the crown would pay every year. Or it would send someone to the warehouse with a knife.
“Who else knows?” Matthias asked.
“The captain of Oswin’s guard saw my hand. He ran off with the cook. Besides him, no one.”
“The moneychangers know you have something to sell.”
“They don’t know what.”
“They’ll guess.” Matthias reached into his belt and drew out a second purse, heavier than the first. He tossed it over. Gernot caught it in both hands, through his sleeves. “Fifty silver now. A ship, when I judge the sea is safe. Until then you sleep at my house.”
“Where?”
“In the upper town, behind walls two ells thick. Cedric won’t look for you in the house of the man he owes.”
Gernot’s shoulders loosened. He clutched the purse and lifted his eyes straight to Matthias. Until now he had looked only at the casks.
“Who will you sell me to?”
“Who says I’ll sell you?”
“You’re Matthias. In Satura they say you buy only what you can sell later for more.”
“In Satura they say a great many things.”
“You’ll sell me to Cedric.”
“If Cedric pays the most, you’ll be worth the most. That ought to cheer you.”
Gernot did not look cheered.
“And the casks?”
“The casks stay here. The seal is His Highness’s.”
Gernot couldn’t tell whether he was being mocked. Matthias didn’t help him decide.
They climbed the stairways of the lower town, past the shuttered fishermen’s taverns. Gernot walked between Matthias and Bran and looked back at every corner, and every time he found Bran’s face behind him. His hands never left his sleeves.
“Where are your gloves?” Matthias asked.
Gernot stumbled on a step.
“How do you know about the gloves?”
“I don’t. Your hands do.”
“They’re still in Satura. In the little room above the cellar, hanging on the nail.” Gernot was quiet for a moment. “I left before dawn and didn’t go back for them.”
A cupbearer who leaves his gloves on the nail is running. Matthias said nothing.
In the square before the grain exchange, the night watchman raised his lantern, recognized Matthias, and drew back against the wall. Gernot saw it. From then on he walked closer to him.
The house in the upper town was dark when they reached it. The steward opened at the first knock, in his shirt with a cloak thrown hastily over it, and did not ask who the guest was. Matthias took the candle from his hand and led Gernot down the stairs to the cellar himself. Bran came down after them.
The steps were stone and slick with damp. Below, it smelled of wine, earth, and oak. The iron door at the far end had a grille set at face height. Behind it, among Matthias’s own casks, lay a straw pallet, a jug of water, bread, and a candle in a clay holder; the steward had laid them out the day before, on an order whose reason he did not know.
“Here?” Gernot asked.
“No one comes in here without me.”
Gernot stepped inside and looked around. He set the purse on the pallet and turned to say something.
Matthias shut the door and turned the key twice.
Bran stood on the bottom step, at the edge of the candlelight. His gaze passed over the purse on the pallet, then settled on the key in Matthias’s hand and stayed there. Matthias paid him no mind.
Half of Ansgar had belonged to him since summer. All of Gernot was his as of tonight.
The cupbearer’s face appeared behind the grille. His hands came out of his sleeves and gripped the bars, bare and white.
“My lord?”
“Alive, you’re a promissory note against the king,” Matthias said. “Dead, you’re just dead weight in my cellar. Keep yourself alive.”
“You said you’d keep me safe.”
“I’m keeping you. No one will find you.”
Gernot watched him through the grille until the candle in Matthias’s hand had burned down a finger’s width. Then his voice grew small.
“And if you go away? Who will feed me?”
Matthias did not answer. He put the key away in his belt and climbed the stairs.
 
Chapter 2: Seven

The bell in the harbor tower was striking midnight when Matthias turned the board around for the third time.
He was playing both sides. He would move white, turn the board, and hunt for black’s best reply against his own white. He was trying to lose. Tonight, like every other night, he failed: the black king fell on the sixteenth move, driven into a corner by a knight and a pawn.
His study was on the top floor. Two flights below, behind the iron door, Gernot was asleep, or was not. No sound made it up through the stone. The house was quiet, the way Matthias liked it: the servants slept in the back wing, the steward in the little room beside the kitchen, and the candle on the writing desk was beeswax and burned without smoke.
He set the pieces up again but did not begin. Instead he took one of the black knights and stood it alone in the middle of the board. The knight was Gernot. Beside it he placed a white pawn, turned facing away: Ansgar, who still believed his note was in Satura. He considered a moment, then added a white bishop on the corner square, where a piece sees the whole diagonal and no one sees it.
The bishop was Isabella. She had found him on her own, without the go-betweens, and had known which terrace he would step out onto. She had asked for eighty and had taken the purse only once he stopped holding it out. And at the very end she had said, “If there’s someone else on the hill.” She had said it in passing.
Matthias touched the bishop with one finger and left it where it stood. He would ask her about the hill later, when her answer would weigh more.
He put the three pieces back in the box and stopped.
His fingers found the cord at his neck on their own and drew out from under his shirt a little pouch of soft leather, darkened with sweat. He untied it. The pebble settled into his palm.
It was gray and smooth, no bigger than his thumbnail, with a thin white vein running through the middle. It held the warmth of his skin.
Thirty years ago Matthias had woken on the shingle beneath the walls of Merante, where the ebb tide leaves its seaweed. He had been a child then, about twelve. He remembered nothing from before: no name, no mother, no notion of how he had come to be on that shore. He remembered only that his right hand had been clenched into a fist and would not open. When at last it did, there was a pebble in it. Matthias had hidden it in his mouth before the fishermen came down to him.
Everything else he had, he had bought: the house, the ships, the silence of his neighbors, the name men greeted him by on the exchange. The pebble he had not bought. No one had ever seen it.
He squeezed it and held his fist closed until the candle had burned down a finger’s width. Then he put it back in the pouch, tied the cord in a double knot, and let the pouch drop beneath his shirt.
He picked up the white queen to begin.
Something pressed behind his eyes.
The candle flame split in two, then into four. The board tilted. Matthias caught the edge of the table with his free hand. The rings on his fingers went cold, then burned.
With the pressure came a warmth that was not his own. It smelled of the hearth and of scorched milk, of dry straw and of blood. Beneath the warmth lay a weariness so old it had become flesh, and a guilt that could never be paid off. And at the very bottom lay hunger. Matthias knew the hunger at once, and at once he saw it was not his. His wanted to have. This one wanted to give, and to be begged for more.
Brother.
The word did not pass through his ears. It settled straight into his mind, whole, like a coin dropped into a cupped hand.
As a boy on the quay, Matthias had listened to the songs of the Vestari, the wanderers who carry news from kingdom to kingdom. They sang of the seven Fallen, the sons who had come down from heaven, and they had a name for each. One they called Mammon, the lover of silver, who had sold heaven for a handful of gold. Matthias had understood even then, at twelve, that the song was about him. He had also understood that it lied. He had never sold anything so cheaply.
The voice came from mind to mind, without air. From where exactly, Matthias could not tell, but he could measure it, and he measured everything. In his mind he leaned toward the warmth, and a thin, hot thread ran from his nose. The warmth on the other side flinched with pain as well. Matthias drew back, and the blood slowed. The closer the other one came, the more they both bled.
He could shut it out. He knew how: the way he shut the door on a seller who wanted too much, quietly and without anger, before the man had named his price. He counted to seven in his head, and the pressure gave by a hair.
He did not shut it out. What lay on the other side was worth something, and Matthias never left goods on the counter before he knew their weight.
He let the pressure stay and began to reckon.
The warmth came from the north, along the coast: farther off than the river mouth and nearer than Satura. On horseback the road was four days. But the one on the other side was lying on cold ground and shrinking from lanterns. A fugitive does not hire horses on a road where every stable knows who has passed. On foot he would need seven days.
Another one. How many of us are there?
The question stood before him like a hand stretched across the counter. Matthias did not take it.
He did not know what he would get in return for the answer. He did not know who was asking, who was with him, who was hunting him, or what he would ask for afterward. The answer was merchandise. Give it away for nothing, and he would be left with nothing to bargain with.
He kept silent.
The blood reached his lip and dripped. It fell onto the white queen in his hand and ran down into the points of her crown.
On the other side the warmth waited. Then voices came through it. He heard them the way the other one heard them, far away in the north: with his ears, in his cold. First came his brother’s voice, hoarse and quiet, asking someone beside him aloud:
“Where do you want to go?”
For a long while nothing came. Then another voice sounded, a man’s, tired and sick, his breath whistling:
“You asked me.”
Silence again. Matthias stood bent over the table with the queen in his fist and did not breathe.
“To Merante.”
Matthias stopped counting. The cold beneath his breastbone went still.
His brother was coming to him. Here, to his own city. That was what he was getting, and he had gotten it for free. Now he could pay.
He set the queen down at the edge of the board. With his other hand he reached into the box of pieces and began taking out the white pawns. He lined them up side by side at his own end of the board, along the first rank. One, two, three. These were the seven Fallen of the song, and he among them. The eighth pawn he left in the box.
Seven, too, were the days on foot from the cold ground in the north to Merante.
A single word said both numbers. He liked that: the other side would get exactly as much as it had asked for, and not a hair more.
Seven.
He said it clearly and drily, and left it there.
The warmth on the other side froze. It held so for the space of a breath drawn and let go. Then the pressure behind his eyes eased, and the voice thinned and was lost. What remained was the silence of the room, the candle, which was one again, and the taste of iron in his mouth.
Matthias straightened. The chair behind him had toppled over, and he could not remember when. He picked it up and set it back in its place.
The white queen lay at the edge of the board, a dark thread dried into the points of her crown. The seven pawns stood in their row.
He went to the washstand in the corner and plunged his hands into the water. It was cold. He splashed his face and watched the pink threads curl in the basin and fade.
Six.
Six besides himself. Five who knew nothing of him, and one who was walking toward him without knowing his name. Matthias ranked them in his head the way he ranked his debtors: by what they could give him and what they could take from him. Of the one in the north he knew two things: that he hungered to give, and that he was a fugitive. A man hungry to give was easily bought. A fugitive dragged his pursuers along behind him.
He would arrive in seven days. With every day they would bleed more.
Matthias looked at the box where the eighth pawn lay and reached for the pouch beneath his shirt. His fingers found the pebble through the leather and closed around it. For thirty years no one had come to him without wanting something. The one in the north would want something too. Everyone does. But he was coming himself rather than sending a go-between, and there was no calculation in his warmth.
He let go of the pouch. His hand had stayed clenched, and he opened it on the table with an effort, one finger at a time.
He had earned his first coin on the same shingle where he had woken: a copper, its face worn smooth, for hauling baskets of fish all day for a fisherman who had no left hand. He did not spend it. He went hungry for two days, and on the third he lent it to another boy from the quay in return for two. By autumn he had six. By spring he had a basket of his own and a smaller boy who hauled the fish in his place. He remembered every one of those coins by the name of its debtor. He remembered the evenings, too: on a dry stretch of the quay, under an upturned boat, with the coins wrapped in a rag beneath his head. No one had ever come under the boat to ask where he was.
His brother would ask. He had asked already: “How many of us are there?” And Matthias had sold him the answer.
He wiped his face with the towel and went back to the table. He picked up the white queen and rubbed her clean with his thumb. The blood would not come out from between the points of her crown.
His hands were shaking. Matthias laid them on the table, palms down, and watched them until they stopped. His throat was dry, and that hoarse voice still lingered in his ears: “Where do you want to go?” His brother had asked someone where he wanted to go. Matthias could not remember anyone ever asking him.
The bell in the harbor tower struck two.
That was when he heard the Chorale.
It came from the street below his windows: men’s voices and one woman’s, slow and restrained, in an old tongue Matthias had never learned yet understood to the last syllable. They sang of the light that burns away the unclean, and of the broom that sweeps the threshold. They were not pleading. They were promising.
At the very first long note, the warmth beneath his breastbone, which had been calling out to gold all evening, shrank and went cold. The rings grew heavy on his fingers. His knees gave, and he braced a palm against the edge of the table. The song had been written for his kind, and it knew where to press.
He did not go to the window. If he showed himself, he would give the voices a face to look at. He stood motionless with wet hands and began to count the voices.
There were seven.
Footsteps sounded on the stairs. The steward came straight in, in his shirtsleeves, a candle in his hand. The candle was trembling.
“My lord. Under the windows…”
“I hear them.”
“Shall I send the lads to drive them off?”
“What do they look like?”
“Gray cloaks, my lord. Hooded. They’re standing in a half circle in front of our door.” The steward swallowed. His gaze caught on Matthias’s collar and darted away. “You’ve been bleeding, my lord.”
“From the nose. The air is dry.”
The steward nodded as if he believed it.
“The baker across the way has lit a candle in a blue cup at his window. So has the tailor. Nobody’s coming out.”
“How many are there?”
The steward blinked.
“I didn’t count.”
“Seven.” Matthias did not turn around. “Bar the shutters. All of them, the ones on the yard too. And tell the lads not to set foot outside till morning.”
The steward hurried off. Soon bolts were rattling all through the house, and the heavy iron bars of the shutters dropped into their hooks one after another. The Chorale did not stop. It came more faintly through the barred shutters and the stone, but it came.
Matthias dried his hands slowly, finger by finger.
It was the Order of Azur singing beneath his windows. In Merante the old folk still called it the Order of the Prophet Elijah, after the man who had founded it, and lit a candle in a blue glass cup whenever its gray brothers passed down the street. The knights of Azur seldom came down to Merante. They kept close to the court in Satura and left the port to the moneychangers. Their choir came down more seldom still. Matthias had heard of it only once. A captain from the northern ports, drunk in his warehouse over the bill for freight, had told how in a village in Avaritia the choir had sung outside the miller’s house all night long. They had not knocked, had not shouted. In the morning the knights carried the miller out in their arms. He was alive, the captain said, and his eyes were open, only he could not shut his mouth. Then they burned him in the village square, and the village brought the kindling. Matthias had paid the freight with a tenth on top so that the captain would not tell that story in other warehouses.
They were singing under his window. Not under Ansgar’s windows, not at the exchange, not on the quay. They knew his house. They knew his face, since they had picked this street out of a hundred streets. And they were singing a song written for the Fallen, which meant they knew what he was as well.
Matthias froze. The cold beneath his breastbone rose and filled his chest, and in the cold his thoughts turned clear.
Someone had sold him. Or the Order had come on its own, the way the tax collector comes, by the ledgers. Which of the two did not matter. What mattered was what they would do. Seven voices were a great many for a song and too few for an assault. They had not come to take him. They had come to tell him they knew.
This was a move. A move was answered with a move.
He went back to the board. The seven white pawns stood in their row along the first rank. Matthias did not put them away. He took the black king from the box and set it in the middle of the board, alone. An opponent always believed a king like that was surrounded. Matthias knew that from the center it could go eight ways, and from the corner only three.
From below, through two flights of stairs and the stone, came a pounding. Dull, uneven, a fist on iron. Gernot had heard the song and did not know whom it was for.
The steward appeared in the doorway again.
“The cupbearer, my lord. He’s beating on the door. Shall I go down and tell him something?”
“What would you tell him?”
The steward did not know. He stood there with the candle and waited.
“Tell him nothing,” Matthias said. “If he shouts, only the cellar will hear him. Tomorrow you’ll take him bread.”
The steward went out. The pounding went on a little longer and stopped. Matthias did not go down. Gernot was in a safe place, and a safe place is not opened in the dead of night, when seven people who know what is in the house are standing under the windows.
He sat down and began to tally in his head what the Order could take. It could take the house, the warehouse on the quay with its six casks, and the two granaries behind the grain exchange. It could take the four ships as well, except that three of them were at sea and could not be sealed. The notes held by the moneychangers in Merante and in Satura lay in the moneychangers’ strongboxes, and a strongbox is the easiest thing of all to seal. All of it could be bought again. What could not, he wore around his neck.
He tried to count to seven. At four the Chorale took up a long note and his counting fell apart. He started over. At five, the same again.
By evening he had to be out of Merante. The decision came of itself, and Matthias nodded to it, to the empty room. His brother would arrive in seven days in a city where Matthias would no longer be. He had told him “Seven” two hours ago, and now that word hung over him like a note he would not be able to pay when it fell due.
